Incorporating NRL Team Lineups into Match
Prediction Systems (2012–Present)
Executive summary
Lineup  information  can  explain  a  large  share  of  match-to-match  variance  that  match-history  features
cannot capture, especially when high-impact spine players appear or withdraw late. The key technical shift
is to treat “team strength” as a sum of (i) a team baseline that evolves over time and (ii) a lineup component
that depends on  who plays and  where they play (starting 13 vs interchange vs reserves), plus uncertainty
because the named squad is not the final 17. 
NRL  lineup  data  exists  in  public,  primary  sources,  but  access  is  shaped  by  platform  constraints  and
licensing. Official club and league sites publish squad lists on a schedule that has changed over time: in
2012 the league described Tuesday lists updated with “Friday morning late mail”, and later communications
in 2017 and 2019 describe 21-man Tuesday squads updated to 19-man 24 hours pre-game and 17-man one
hour pre-kick-off; a 2026 club post shows a 22-man Tuesday squad at 4pm. 
The most robust modelling pattern for your setting (games 2012–present; existing match-history features)
is:
(1) build a canonical historical lineup table (match_id × player_id × team_id × listed_position × status),
(2)  estimate  player  and  position  effects  with  strong  regularisation  or  Bayesian  shrinkage  to  avoid
overfitting,
(3) integrate “Thursday inference” by marginalising over uncertain final selections using historical selection
probabilities from named squads, and
(4) validate with time-respecting splits, calibration, and ablations that isolate what lineups add beyond team
form and head-to-head history. 
Reinforcement learning (RL) is useful once you can (a) simulate counterfactual lineups or (b) frame selection
and interchange decisions as policies with measurable rewards. Given rugby league data scarcity, offline RL
is the relevant regime: it needs careful off-policy evaluation (OPE) and conservative policy improvement to
avoid learning actions unsupported by historical data. 
Lineups in the NRL data lifecycle
Squad announcements are not a single event. The data product you need depends on whether you predict
on Tuesday, Thursday, or just before kick-off. In 2012, league posts describe weekly team lists “announced
first every Tuesday” and “updated with the Friday morning late mail”.   A club explainer from 2017
describes a three-stage process: 21 players named Tuesday (4pm AEST), reduced to a 19-man squad 24
hours pre-game, then final 17 confirmed one hour pre-kick-off, with limited exceptions approved by the NRL
Head of Football.  A league post from 2019 repeats the 21-man → 19-man → 17-man cadence explicitly
for that round.

The 2026 season shows that these rules can shift. A 2026 club post names a 22-man squad at 4pm Tuesday
and  publishes  a  structured  squad  list  including  “Reserves”  beyond  the  interchange.   This  variation
matters operationally: a production system should not hard-code “21-man Tuesday squads” as an invariant,
and should store squad size and role labels as observed, season-by-season. 
“Lineup effect” is not just “player present”. Rugby league roles are asymmetric: the spine concentrates
decision-making and ball touches, interchange timing changes matchups, and late positional switches can
change a player’s contribution even if the player remains selected. Rugby league performance research has
already operationalised position-specific modelling, including separate distributions and models by position
group and explicit modelling of interchange roles. 
Licensing constraints shape what “public lineup data” means. League and club websites operate under
terms that restrict use to personal, non-commercial contexts and limit reproduction without consent, which
creates compliance risk for persistent scraping or redistribution.  In parallel, commercial data providers
exist: a 2018 press release states that STATS (now part of Stats Perform) signed a multi-year agreement as
the official data provider and distributes live and post-match data via data feeds to partners.   Rugby
league  research  using  event-level  or  detailed  performance  feeds  frequently  notes  license  and  ethics
constraints that prevent sharing raw data, which is a practical indicator that “full-fidelity” datasets are
typically commercial rather than open. 
Data sources and acquisition strategy
Primary sources that cover 2012–present lineups exist, but each source is strongest for a different “truth”:
named squads, final 17s, positional labels, and last-minute changes.
A practical design is to store multiple lineup “versions” per match (e.g., named squad, 24-hour squad, final
17, actual minute participation where available), then let modelling choose which version to condition on
depending on inference time. The monitoring layer should follow the publishing cadence and treat each
fetch as an append-only event.

Near-term capture for “Thursday inference”
A robust design treats Thursday as “latest reliable snapshot before many weekend matches”, not as a
universal squad release time. In the 2017 description, the cut to 19 happens 24 hours pre-game, which
means Wednesday for Thursday matches, Thursday for Friday matches, and so on.   The 2019 league
post makes the same “24 hours before each game” statement. 
Operationally, a weekly job should:
Pull the nominated squads as soon as they publish (historically Tuesday; in at least one 2026 club
post this is timestamped 4pm). 
Pull the 24-hour updates per match in a match-aware schedule keyed off fixture times. 
Optionally pull the final 17 one hour pre-kick-off when latency allows, producing “late model refresh”
predictions.

Literature review on lineup and player-impact modelling
Rugby league has less public research on lineup-aware win probability than sports with rich public play-by-
play, but several strands transfer directly.
Rugby league research has already built player contribution metrics tied to team success from multi-season
professional data. One open-access study in Research Quarterly for Exercise and Sport (men’s rugby league,
2018–2022 seasons) uses multiple machine learning algorithms and constructs “Wins Created”, “Losses
Created”, and “Net Wins Added”; it reports strong match-winner classification performance and validates
against  awards  and  representative  selection.   A  doctoral  thesis  in  Australia  reports  a  broader
programme: literature review, player metrics in men’s and women’s rugby league, a match outcome model
that incorporates lineup composition with team attributes (including an Elo component), and a decision
support system that optimises lineups under constraints like availability and positional eligibility. 
Expected-points and expected-possession-value (EPV) modelling in rugby league provides a mechanism to
create  denser  rewards  than  match  outcome  alone.  Work  using  2010–2013  NRL  play-by-play  estimated
expected point outcomes by field location and possession context, highlighting how possession origin and
field position change try likelihood and expected points.  Later work in rugby league uses Markov chain
framing of sets and produces EPV zone models, explicitly discussing data licensing restrictions when using
Stats Perform style feeds.   Bayesian EPV variants model smooth pitch surfaces and use hierarchical
priors to share information between league and team levels, which mirrors the same partial pooling logic
needed for player effects. 
In other invasion sports, lineup and player impact models largely fall into four families:
Adjusted plus-minus (APM) and its regularised variants estimate player contributions by regressing stint
outcomes on who was on the field, addressing teammate and opponent confounding; regularisation is
central to stability and out-of-sample behaviour .   Soccer plus-minus work adapts the concept to low-
scoring settings and introduces expected-goals-based plus-minus to reduce goal noise.  Rating-system
extensions (Elo/Glicko) add uncertainty and dynamic updates; Glicko explicitly introduces rating deviation as
a reliability measure beyond Elo.  Bayesian hierarchical models for match outcomes are widely used in
football result prediction and provide a principled route to partial pooling across teams, seasons, and
competitions.   Representation learning (embeddings, graph models) encodes player interactions and
co-occurrence in compact vectors; recent work explicitly describes swapping player embeddings to analyse
how personnel changes affect match outcome predictions. 
Causal inference has become more explicit in sports analytics, including player evaluation. A causality-
inspired APM model defines player value as a counterfactual score change under substitution, aligning with
the core question “what is the effect of player X being in the team?”.   Broader causal sports work
emphasises that different estimands (ATE vs ATT) and matching/weighting designs change interpretation,
which is directly relevant when player selection is non-random.

Handling uncertainty: named squads vs the final 17
A match prediction made on Thursday should rarely assume the named squad equals the final 17, because
selection rules and update windows explicitly permit changes up to one hour pre-kick-off.  Your model
can represent this uncertainty explicitly:
Fit a selection model: . 
Fit an outcome model conditioned on a realised final 17. 
Marginalise: , approximated via Monte Carlo over
plausible final lineups. 
The 2017 description of selection stages gives direct structure for this two-stage approach (21/19/17 with
limited exceptions).  The 2026 club example shows that even the named squad size can differ (22-man),
so the selection model should condition on observed squad size rather than assuming it. 
Matchup and interaction features that are specific to rugby league
Rugby league player contribution is context dependent. The men’s rugby league metric study explicitly splits
variables  by  position  and  treats  offence  and  defence  separately,  indicating  that  position-conditioned
modelling is not optional if you want stable estimates.   Practical lineup features that align with this
include:
position-weighted player ratings (spine vs middles vs edges vs outside backs), 
“spine continuity” features (same 1/6/7/9 combination as last match, last month), 
bench utility composition (e.g., presence of a hooker on bench) and interchange depth, 
team-level strength-of-schedule and opponent-strength adjustments when estimating player form.

Expected points / EPV models provide a principled way to turn play and field position into a denser signal
than win/loss, which can stabilise player and lineup estimates when match counts per season are modest.
Reinforcement learning for lineup optimisation and sequential
decisions
Two RL problem shapes matter in rugby league: pre-match selection and in-match interchange.
Pre-match selection is closer to a contextual bandit: you observe a match context (opponent, venue, travel,
injuries,  recent  form),  pick  a  lineup  action,  then  observe  a  reward  (points  differential,  win  probability
change, or match result). The doctoral thesis described a decision support system that chooses optimal
lineups under constraints such as availability and positional eligibility, which corresponds to a constrained
decision problem even if implemented without RL. 
In-match interchange is a sequential decision problem. The true physiological state (fatigue, knocks) is
partially observed, so a POMDP is a closer formalisation than a fully observed MDP . Standard RL references
define MDPs and POMDPs and show how belief states act as sufficient statistics under partial observability.
RL formulation and reward design
A useful modelling split is:
State: time, score differential, current on-field lineup, interchange remaining, recent possession
states, and latent fatigue proxy. 
Action: substitution decisions (who off, who on), or pre-match roster selection. 
Reward: dense rewards derived from expected points / EPV plus terminal match outcome, to reduce
sparsity. Rugby league expected-points and EPV work shows that possession context and field
position map to expected point equity, which naturally supports reward shaping. 
Offline RL is the default regime because you cannot safely or ethically explore random interchange policies
in the real competition. Offline RL surveys and tutorials describe the “distribution shift” problem: learned
policies can choose actions not supported by the dataset, causing extrapolation error , so conservative
learning  and  safe  policy  improvement  matter .   Off-policy  evaluation  methods  like  doubly  robust
estimators are central when you want to evaluate a new policy using historical data logged under different
behaviour policies. 
Empirical work applying offline RL to professional basketball tactics indicates the feasibility of training
policies from logged data, but that setting typically has richer possession-level data and more frequent
decisions than rugby league, so transfer should be cautious.

Simulation vs model-based planning
Planning  methods  need  a  transition  model.  In  sports,  “world  models”  often  approximate  how  actions
change win probability or expected outcome rather than simulate every micro-event. Action valuation work
in football frames possessions with value functions, including deep RL approaches that estimate action-
values  from  event  or  tracking  data.   Rugby  league  EPV  models  already  treat  sets  as  Markovian
sequences, which is compatible with building a dynamics model at the level of field position and possession
outcomes, even without full tracking. 
Practical pipeline, validation, and implementation roadmap
The pipeline should separate three concerns: (i) ingestion and canonicalisation, (ii) feature construction at a
chosen “as-of time”, and (iii) modelling and evaluation.

Training, validation, and evaluation metrics
A time-ordered evaluation is essential because team rosters, rules, and playing styles shift across 2012–
present. Rugby league research already reports order-preserved testing as a distinct evaluation mode for
match prediction models. 
A defensible protocol:
Primary split: rolling-origin evaluation (train through season t, test on season t+1), repeated for
multiple years. 
Secondary split: within-season forecasting (train up to round r, test on rounds r+1…), matching
“Thursday inference” operational reality.

Metrics: log loss and Brier score for probabilistic accuracy; reliability diagrams / calibration error;
and discrimination metrics (AUC) as secondary. These are standard for probabilistic forecasts and
align with the “win probability” output target. 
Missing data, late changes, and player movement
Late changes should not be treated as noise. The official selection cadence explicitly allows changes near
kick-off and, in limited cases, outside the named squad for approved reasons.   Store every observed
update  as  a  timestamped  lineup  version,  and  train  models  to  condition  on  “as-of  time”  because  the
information set differs materially between Tuesday, Thursday, and match-day. 
Transfers and position changes create identifiability problems if you model “player value” as static. Partial
pooling helps: hierarchical priors shrink uncertain player effects toward position and team baselines, and
time-varying components can capture form changes. This is consistent with how Bayesian hierarchical
sports models handle heterogeneity and how causal APM frames substitution effects under uncertainty.
Recommended experiments and ablations
A small set of ablations can isolate what lineup information adds:
Baseline match-history model vs match-history + “named squad only” features at the same cut-off
time. 
Replace binary “player present” with position-weighted player ratings and compare calibration and
log loss. 
Deterministic final-17 assumption vs probabilistic marginalisation over possible final 17s (Monte
Carlo). 
Additivity-only vs additivity + limited interaction terms (spine pairings), with strong regularisation, to
test whether synergy is learnable without overfit. 
Sensitivity analysis: causal-inspired adjustment (weighting/matching) for a small set of star-player
treatments to test confounding magnitude.

Deployment considerations: latency, compute, and monitoring
The dataset scale is modest: 2012–present yields only a few thousand matches, so model training is not
compute-bound  unless  you  add  heavy  neural  components.  The  operational  risk  is  data  freshness  and
schema  breakage,  not  GPU  time.  Community  tooling  explicitly  emphasises  rate  limiting,  caching,  and
handling missing elements as first-class engineering issues, which matches the realities of lineup scraping.
“Thursday inference” latency is dominated by ingestion (waiting for squad updates) rather than feature
computation. A production system benefits from two inference modes: (i) scheduled refresh keyed to the
24-hour update windows, and (ii) rapid re-score when last-minute lineup changes publish. The selection-
stage descriptions justify this design. 
Compliance is a non-technical blocker . The league site’s terms include non-commercial use language and
restrictions on reproducing site material without consent, which is incompatible with broad redistribution of
scraped lineups. If predictions are used beyond personal research, licensing discussions with official data
providers are the cleanest route.
